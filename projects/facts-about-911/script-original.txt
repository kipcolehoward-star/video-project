# 1. THE ORIGIN OF 911

**VISUAL:** 1960s America. Old telephones. Telephone operators. Police cars.

**NARRATOR:**

> The story of 911 begins in the 1960s.

> Back then, there was no single emergency number covering the United States.

> If you needed the police, fire department, or an ambulance, you generally had to know and dial the local telephone number for the appropriate service.

> And that created a serious problem.

**VISUAL:** Person frantically searching through a telephone book.

> Imagine witnessing a serious accident.

> Someone is injured.

> There's a fire.

> And you have to stop and figure out which number to call.

> Every second matters.

**ON SCREEN:**  
**POLICE → LOCAL NUMBER**  
**FIRE → LOCAL NUMBER**  
**AMBULANCE → LOCAL NUMBER**

> The idea of a single emergency number wasn't completely new.

> In fact, Britain had already introduced **999** as an emergency number in 1937.

> In the United States, the idea gained momentum during the 1950s and 1960s.

> In 1967, the President's Commission on Law Enforcement and Administration of Justice recommended creating a single number for reporting emergencies.

> The Federal Communications Commission then worked with AT&T to find a number that could be implemented across the country.

# 2. WHY 911?

**VISUAL:** Old rotary telephone. Digits 9 → 1 → 1 highlighted.

**NARRATOR:**

> So why did they choose 911?

> AT&T selected the number because it was short, easy to remember, and—critically—had not already been assigned as an area code, office code, or service code in the telephone system.

> It also fit the technology of the time.

> Remember, these were the days of rotary phones.

**VISUAL:** Hand slowly dialing a rotary phone.

> The number needed to be simple enough to remember and quick enough to dial during an emergency.

**ON SCREEN:**

### SHORT
### MEMORABLE
### QUICK TO DIAL
### UNIQUE

# 3. WHAT HAPPENED BEFORE 911?

**VISUAL:** 1950s/60s telephone operator.

**NARRATOR:**

> Before 911, one common option was to dial **0** and reach a telephone operator.

> The operator could then help connect the caller to the appropriate emergency service.

> But this wasn't a dedicated emergency system.

> It depended on the operator, local telephone infrastructure, and knowing where the appropriate emergency service was.

> As emergency calls increased and society became more dependent on telephones, the limitations became increasingly obvious.

> Fire officials had already been pushing for a simpler emergency number.

> In 1957, the National Association of Fire Chiefs reportedly recommended a single number for reporting fires.

**VISUAL:** Old newspaper headlines / fire alarm boxes / telephone.

> The goal was simple:

**ON SCREEN:**

### ONE NUMBER.
### ONE CALL.
### GET HELP FAST.

# 4. THE FIRST 911 CALL

**VISUAL:** Map of Alabama → Haleyville.

**NARRATOR:**

> Then came February 16th, 1968.

> In Haleyville, Alabama, something happened that would eventually change emergency communications across America.

**SFX:** Telephone ring.

> Alabama Speaker of the House **Rankin Fite** picked up a telephone and dialed 911.

> The call was answered by U.S. Representative Tom Bevill at the local police station.

> It was the first official 911 call in the United States.

**ON SCREEN:**

### FEBRUARY 16, 1968
### HALEYVILLE, ALABAMA

> But here's something important:

> 911 didn't instantly become available everywhere in America.

> The system took years to expand.

> Different communities and telephone companies had to build the infrastructure necessary to route emergency calls.

> Eventually, 911 became the standard Americans know today.

# 5. WHAT ACTUALLY PROMPTED ITS CREATION?

**VISUAL:** Montage of car crashes, fires, ambulances, police.

**NARRATOR:**

> So what actually prompted the creation of 911?

> There wasn't one single event.

> It was the growing realization that emergency communication needed to become faster, simpler, and more reliable.

> The United States was experiencing growing numbers of emergencies, including serious injuries from automobile crashes.

> A landmark 1966 report from the National Academy of Sciences highlighted accidental death and injury as a major public-health problem and called for exploring a single nationwide telephone number to summon an ambulance.

> At the same time, police and fire officials were pushing for simpler ways for the public to report emergencies.

> In other words…

**ON SCREEN:**

### 911 WAS CREATED TO SOLVE A COMMUNICATION PROBLEM.

**VISUAL:** United States flag → "911"  
**TRANSITION:** United Kingdom flag → "999"

**NARRATOR:**

> Now let's cross the Atlantic.

> The United States uses **911**.

> The United Kingdom uses **999**.

> So are they basically the same thing?

> In purpose, yes.

> Both are emergency numbers designed to connect people with emergency services.

> But they are different numbers belonging to different national telephone systems.

**ON SCREEN:**

🇺🇸 UNITED STATES  
### 911

🇬🇧 UNITED KINGDOM  
### 999

> And here's another interesting fact:

> The UK also supports **112**, the pan-European emergency number.

> So if you're in Britain, you don't normally dial 911.

> You dial **999** or **112** for emergencies.

**VISUAL:** Phone screen showing:

### UK EMERGENCY NUMBERS
**999**
**112**

> So if an American tourist in London instinctively dials 911…

> That's not the number they should rely on.

# 8. WHICH COUNTRIES USE 911?

**VISUAL:** World map. Countries light up.

**NARRATOR:**

> So, is 911 only used in the United States?

> No.

> A number of countries and territories use 911 as an emergency number, either as their primary emergency number or alongside other numbers.

> Examples include **Canada, Mexico, the Philippines, American Samoa, Antigua and Barbuda, and several Caribbean countries.**

> But there's an important warning:

> Don't assume that 911 works everywhere just because it works in the United States.

> Emergency numbers vary dramatically around the world.

**ON SCREEN:**

USA  
Canada

Philippines  
Several Caribbean territories

> And some countries support multiple emergency numbers.

> For example, the United Kingdom uses **999 and 112**.
